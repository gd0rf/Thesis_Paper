\section{Introduction}
Space has been at the forefront of research and exploration for decades and much of modern technology has come to rely on the inovations and architecture thats been built to maintain it. The largest growing component of space innovation involves the deployment of \ac{LEO} satellites\cite{zhangLEOMegaConstellations2022}. This greatly expanding font of new technology and ideas brings along its own challenges that range from security to new ways to track orbits \cite{idanAegisSatSatelliteCybersecurity2025,kazemiOrbitDeterminationSpace2024}. 


\section{CubeSats}
The use of CubeSats has continued to grow in the \ac{LEO} enviroment and has become a major component of the space industry\cite{zhangLEOMegaConstellations2022}. CubeSats are small satellites that are typically built in a standard size and form factor, which allows for easy deployment and integration into existing satellite constellations. This standardization has led to the rapid growth of CubeSat technology including the development of new communication systems\cite{saeedCubeSatCommunicationsRecent2020,rastinasabImplementationDesignRFFrontEnd2021} and onboard processing capabilities\cite{sajjadDesigningReliableLowPower2024}. These developments have enabled CubeSats to perform a wide range of missions, from Earth observation, scientific research, and commercial applications.

\subsection{Security}
With the rapid growth of CubeSat technology, there has been an increased focus on the vulnerabilities and security risks associated with these small satellites. Much of these now deployed CubeSats are built with a focus on cost and size much like IoT devices, which has led to a lack of security features and protocols\cite{marraFeasibilityCubeSatsApplication2024}. This makes CubeSats a prime target for research into security and resiliancy of these systems.

Ahmad et al.\cite{ahmadGANBasedApproachEnhancing2025} pursure the one tactic of utilizing a \ac{GAN}, a type of neural network, to generate synthetic data to train a \ac{ML} model to detect and mitigate cyber attacks on CubeSats. It utilized the Edge-IIoTset to train the \ac{GAN} to generate the sythetic data and the used the generated data to train a \ac{CNN} plus an autoencoder to detect cyber attacks on IoT devices speceficially aimed at CubeSats. The results displayed a validation accuracy of 96.56\% for the \ac{CNN} in the first expoch showing that the \ac{GAN} was able to generate realistic data that could be used to train a \ac{ML} model to detect cyber attacks on CubeSats. This demonstrates the potential of using \ac{GAN}s to enhance the security of CubeSats and other small satellites. 
%link to other defensive papers

In the offensive space security domain Calabrese et al.\cite{calabreseHostedPayloadCyber2024} explores the use of a hosted payload to perform a cyber attack against a CubeSate in a simulated enviroment. The study provides a full demonstratable attack chain, inspired by the SPARTA Matrix\cite{aerospaceSPARTA}, the only uses open source tools and software to demonstarate the vulnerabilities of CubeSats and the potential for cyber attacks against them.

\section{Navigation}
\ac{LEO} satellite use a variety of methods to assist in their own navigation and an organizartions ability to track them from the ground. The most common methods used to track a satellite form their ground is through \ac{TLE}s. \ac{TLE}s describe the orbital parameters, as seen in Table~\ref{acro:TLE}, that are required for the tracking and locating of specefic satellites during orbit\cite{TwolineElementSets2012}. They provide the specefic time and location that each \ac{LEO} satellite ocuppies to facilitate communication and to ensure that no satellite impacts another. The recordings of a satellites orbit are taken through observation at \ac{JSPOC} and provide consistent snapshot updates of currently recorded TLE's every 8 hours\cite{TwolineElementSets2012}. 
A majority of these satellites have a mean motion of 90 minutes with an alititude(Circular) of 281.49 km\cite{spacetrack2026}. This provides only a few minutes in which the satellite can be contacted before it is out of range and continues until its next orbit. These minimal contact intervals make it vitally important to accuratly predict when contact can be made and what communications are of import to send during this window. This creates a need for accurate truly autnomous navigation and orbit determination that does not rely on outside systems to provide accurate information\cite{psiakiGroundTestsMagnetometerbased1993}.

\section{Alternative Naviagtion}
Alternative methods of navigation and orbit determination continue to be explored to provide more robust and accurate systems. A newly explored source of navigation is the use of satellite sensors, like magnetomoeter and gravitometer, in conjunction with accurate modeling to derive orbit parameters without specefic ground station interference\cite{finlayCHAOS7GeomagneticField2020,sunLowEarthOrbitDetermination2016, butcherKalmanFilteringObservability2017}. These alternative methods provide an independent system of determination without reliance on outside systems\cite{butcherKalmanFilteringObservability2017}.

\subsection{Magnetometer}
It has been proven by Psiaki and Martel\cite{psiakiAutonomousMagneticNavigation} that 5 of the 6 keplerian elements can be determined using only the magnetic field with the missing element being the longitude of the ascending node. Utilizing the magnetometer, known variables like start postion and time of flight along with a series of kalman filters the orbit/position of a satellite can be determined\cite{ningAnalysisFilteringMethods2011,deutschmannEvaluationAttitudeOrbit2000}. 

Pisaki and Martel\cite{psiakiGroundTestsMagnetometerbased1993} utilize a \ac{SRIF} and \ac{BLS} to determine the position of the satellite using only the magnetometer and adjusted based on the truth data. The \ac{SRIF} was tested on the \ac{MAGSAT} launched in October 1979 equipped with a vector magnetometer, the \ac{DE-2} launched in August 1981 equipped with vector magnetometer, and the \ac{LACE} launched in February 1990 equipped with a scalar magnetometer. The results form the tests can be seen in Table~\ref{tab:magnav_results} where the \ac{SRIF} was able to provide a position estimate with a peak error of less than 8 km for the \ac{MAGSAT} and the \ac{BLS} was able to provide a position estimate with a \ac{RMS} error of 4 km for the \ac{MAGSAT}, 18 km for the \ac{DE-2}, and 88 km for the \ac{LACE}\cite{psiakiGroundTestsMagnetometerbased1993}. This demonstrates that the magnetometer can be used to provide a position estimate with a reasonable accuracy, but it is not as accurate as \ac{GNSS} and is dependent on the type of magnetometer used.

A series of kalman filters have been explored by Ning and Ma\cite{ningAnalysisFilteringMethods2011} to determine the method and accuracy of differing strategies, this inclued \ac{UKF}, \ac{EKF}, and \ac{UPF}. A best case \ac{RMS} accuracy of 203.32 meters for an \ac{EKF}, a \ac{RMS} accuracy of 161.3 meters for an \ac{UKF}, and a \ac{RMS} accuracy of 159.7 meters for an \ac{UPF} when considering a known orbit  and 3 second sample rate to filter to a position\cite{ningAnalysisFilteringMethods2011}. The \ac{EKF} introduces a larger error due to the neglected non-linearities in the system, while the \ac{UPF} provides a more accurate result at the cost of increased computational complexity\cite{ningAnalysisFilteringMethods2011}. While this method does not provide the same accuracy of \ac{GNSS} it does provide an independent method of orbit determination that if given the compute power and time can provide a redundant system of orbit determination without reliance on outside systems.

\subsection{Gravitometer}
A further exploration of alternative navigation methods has been explored by Sun et aL~\cite{sunLowEarthOrbitDetermination2016}. This study explores the use of an \ac{ILS}, an \ac{ULS}, and the proposed \ac{HLS} utilizing gracity gradient measurements to determine specefic orbital elements of a satellite. These filters were implimented on the Gravity Field and Steady-State Ocean Circulation Explorers 12 hour arc starting from 8 September 2013, 00:00:00 GPS time. This study also makes the assumption that the gravity gradient measurements are optimal and available. A baseline gravity model EGM2008 is used to provide refrence and as a comparison to the EGM96, JGM3, EIGEN-6C4, and GGM05G models~\ref{tab:gravity_model_comparison}. The resilt of the study shows the \ac{HLS} model provides the best results with a \ac{RMS} error of 10.4 meters in the radial position, 677 meters in the along-track position, and 22.8 meters in the cross-track position~\ref{tab:filter_results}.
